TEACHING COPY BEGINS — Production notes remain non-printing 3.3 Direct Proportion When Brenda made enough cake for three times as many people, she used three times as much of every ingredient. Keeping the same recipe kept the proportions the same. Both amounts changed by the same factor: 4 × 3 = 12 people, and 3 × 3 = 9 cups of flour. This is direct proportion. The amount for one person stays the same. Let’s use that “one first” idea with cookies. END PANEL CH03-PAGE038-PANEL01 |

Let’s work it through We are making cookies of the same size using the same recipe. So each cookie needs the same amount of oatmeal. First find that amount for one cookie, then make 24 of those equal amounts.  DO NOT PRINT — Figure reference V15. The following caption sentence is reader-facing. Divide both quantities by 16, then multiply both by 24. Production note V15 — Solution diagram: two aligned columns headed Cookies and Oatmeal. Rows 16/128 g, 1/8 g and 24/192 g. Between first and second rows place ÷16 arrows on both columns; between second and third place ×24 arrows on both. Include one recognisable cookie beside the middle row, not an uncounted decorative group. Use the current ChandRush colour and design style wherever it supports understanding. If that style would reduce clarity, retain the teaching colours, contrast and mathematical design required here. Mathematical accuracy and comprehension take priority. Oatmeal for 1 cookie = 128 ÷ 16 = 8 g Oatmeal for 24 cookies = 8 × 24 = 192 g Ahmad needs 192 g of oatmeal. Check: 192 ÷ 24 = 8 g per cookie, the same as 128 ÷ 16. More cookies need more oatmeal, but each cookie’s share stays 8 g. Finding one item first is often called the unitary method. “One first” is the idea to remember. Direct proportion needs that unchanged amount per item; growing older, for instance, does not make your height double whenever your age doubles. END PANEL CH03-PAGE038-PANEL02 |
