END NON-PRINTING CARRIED PRODUCTION CONTEXT ORIGINAL PANEL CONTENT Practice 3A Question 6 Simon’s height is 1.67 m and his weight is 43 kg. He claims that the ratio of his height to his weight is 1.67 m : 43 kg. Explain whether he is correct. Check your method Simon is not making a same-kind comparison: 1.67 m measures length, while 43 kg measures mass. Changing metres to centimetres would still leave length compared with mass. We cannot turn both into one matching unit. For the ratios we are simplifying here, compare lengths with lengths or masses with masses. Comparisons between different kinds, such as kilometres per litre, keep their units and need a different interpretation. END PANEL CH03-PAGE014-PANEL01 |

END NON-PRINTING CARRIED PRODUCTION CONTEXT ORIGINAL PANEL CONTENT Practice 3A Question 7 Joseph and Nurul have numbers of balls in the ratio 2 : 3. They are each given an equal number of extra balls. They claim that their ratio is still 2 : 3. (a) Is this claim possible? Use specific numbers to support your answer. (b) What do you learn from this? Check your method (a) Suppose Joseph starts with 2 balls and Nurul with 3. Give each 1 more ball. Joseph now has 2 + 1 = 3; Nurul has 3 + 1 = 4. The new ratio is 3 : 4, not 2 : 3. Their difference stays 1 ball. To have the ratio 2 : 3 with more balls, both amounts would have to grow by the same factor: for example, 4 : 6 has a difference of 2 balls. Equal additions cannot do that. If both receive some extra balls, their 2 : 3 comparison changes. (b) Adding the same number to both terms does not generally preserve a ratio. Multiplying or dividing both terms by the same positive number does. Let’s now see why that works. END PANEL CH03-PAGE014-PANEL02 |
