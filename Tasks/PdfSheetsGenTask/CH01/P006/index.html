1.1 Four Operations on Integers

Let’s get comfortable with numbers on both sides of zero. We’ll learn to add, subtract, multiply and divide with them, starting with a picture of where they belong.

Imagine a lift with a G button for the ground floor and a −1 button for the basement immediately below it. If the lift also used a 0 button, which button could 0 replace?

Check your thinking

0 could replace G. The ground floor is our starting level: one floor above it is 1; one floor below it is −1. The minus sign tells us which side of zero we mean. It does the same job in a temperature such as −5°C.

Integers on a number line

The numbers …, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, … are called integers. They include negative integers, zero and positive integers. Zero is neither positive nor negative. A fraction such as one-half is not an integer. We usually leave off the + sign for a positive number: +4 and 4 mean the same thing. Keep the − in −4, though—it changes the number!

Move your eye to the right along the line: the numbers get greater. That is why 4 < 6 and −3 > −5. The symbol < means “is less than”; > means “is greater than”. A number can be negative and still be greater than another negative number: −3 sits to the right of −5.

The equals sign means “has the same value as”: 1 + 2 = 3 says that the two sides have equal value. The symbol ≠ means “is not equal to”: −1 ≠ 1 because −1 and 1 are different numbers.
