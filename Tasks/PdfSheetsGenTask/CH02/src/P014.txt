# 2.1 Improper Fractions and Mixed Numbers
The rectangles in the next picture stand for pies. Each full rectangle is 1 whole pie, cut into 2 equal halves. A shaded half-rectangle means 1/2 of a pie.
On the left, we have 1 whole pie and 1/2 of another pie. Let’s count how many half-pies that makes.
[ProductionLabel] DO NOT PRINT — Figure reference: V03 | The following caption sentence is printable unchanged.
[Caption] 1 whole pie gives 2 halves; another half makes 3 halves.
[ProductionNote] Production note V03 — Show identical wholes. First state has one complete whole and one half; second state shows exactly three half-pieces. Label 1 1/2 and 3/2. Keep the reference whole fixed. Use the current ChandRush colour and design style wherever it supports understanding. If that style would reduce clarity, retain the teaching colours, contrast and mathematical design required here. Mathematical accuracy and comprehension take priority.
The first whole pie gives us 2 halves: 1 = 2/2.
The second pie gives us 1 more half: 1/2.
Count the halves: 2 + 1 = 3 half-pies.
So 1 1/2 = 2/2 + 1/2 = 3/2.
Read that as “one and a half pies is the same amount as three half-pies”. We have not gained or lost any pie.
Look at 3/2. The bottom number, 2, tells us that each whole pie is cut into 2 equal parts. The top number, 3, tells us that we have 3 of those parts. The bottom number is called the denominator; the top number is the numerator.
A fraction such as 1/2 or 3/4 is less than 1 whole. Its top number is smaller than its bottom number. We call it a proper fraction.
A fraction such as 3/2 is more than 1 whole. The fraction 4/4 is exactly 1 whole. When the top number is greater than or equal to the bottom number, we call it an improper fraction. “Improper” does not mean the fraction has misbehaved!
The number 1 1/2 has a whole-number part, 1, and a fraction part, 1/2. That is why we call it a mixed number. It means 1 + 1/2, not 1 × 1/2.
