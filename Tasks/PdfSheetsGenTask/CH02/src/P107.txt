## Negative Decimals
So far, most of our decimals have been above zero. Decimals can also describe positions below zero. Think of a thermometer: −15.8°C is 15.8 degrees below zero. The minus sign belongs to the number; it tells us which side of zero the value lies on.

On the number line, −0.5 lies to the left of −0.1, so −0.5 < −0.1. Positive numbers lie to the right of 0; negative numbers lie to the left of 0. Zero itself is neither positive nor negative.
A bank balance of −$0.80 means $0.80 is owed. A sea bed 1.38 km below sea level has height −1.38 km when sea level is labelled 0 km. In both cases, the minus sign tells us the value is below the chosen zero.
Adding a negative number moves us left. For example, start at −2 and add −3: move 3 places left to −5. We write −2 + (−3) = −5.
Subtracting a negative number moves us right. For −2 − (−3), move 3 places right from −2: pass −1, then 0, then reach 1. So −2 − (−3) = −2 + 3 = 1. The decimal examples below use these same directions.
On a calculator, the key that makes a number negative, often (−) or +/−, may differ from the subtraction key. For −2.03 − (−5.92), you must enter both negative numbers and the subtraction between them. Check the full expression on the display.
