[TeacherBridge] Four plates are waiting. Let’s see how division counts servings, starting with apples before we return to the batter.
[PrintField] TEACHING COPY BEGINS — ALL SUPPLIED LEARNER CONTENT FOLLOWS; PRODUCTION NOTES REMAIN NON-PRINTING
For division, ask how many groups we can make. Suppose we have 6 apples and put 2 apples in each group. Then 6 ÷ 2 = 3 groups.
Now let each serving be 1/2 of an apple. Each apple gives 2 servings, so 6 apples give 6 × 2 = 12 servings. We write 6 ÷ 1/2 = 12.
Each row in the next diagram represents the same 6 apples. A length of 1 stands for 1 apple. The colours mark the groups: 2 apples per group in the first row, 1/2 apple per group in the second row. We will use the third row next.

[Caption] Divide 6 apples into groups of 2, 1/2 or 2/3 apple.

Now look at the third row: each serving is 2/3 of an apple. Cut every apple into 3 equal parts. From 6 apples we get 6 × 3 = 18 one-third pieces.
Each serving needs 2 of those pieces, so 18 ÷ 2 = 9 servings.
6 ÷ 2/3 = (6 × 3) ÷ 2 = 18 ÷ 2 = 9.
The calculation multiplied by 3, then divided by 2. We can write those two operations as multiplying by 3/2:
6 × 3/2 = (6 × 3)/2 = 18/2 = 9.
The fraction 3/2 is called the reciprocal of 2/3. To find it, swap the top and bottom numbers: 2 over 3 becomes 3 over 2.
Check: 2/3 × 3/2 = (2 × 3)/(3 × 2) = 6/6 = 1. Any non-zero number multiplied by its reciprocal gives 1. Zero has no reciprocal.
So 6 ÷ 2/3 = 6 × 3/2. The starting number, 6, stays unchanged. Only the fraction we divide by, 2/3, is turned over. Let’s use the same idea when the starting amount is a fraction.
