## Comparing Improper Fractions or Mixed Numbers
Example 1
[Question] Which is smaller, 4/3 or 11/9?
Let’s work it through
[WorkedStep] Let’s find the whole-number parts first. We have already practised this conversion:
[WorkedStep] 4/3: 4 ÷ 3 = 1 remainder 1, so 4/3 = 1 1/3.
[WorkedStep] 11/9: 11 ÷ 9 = 1 remainder 2, so 11/9 = 1 2/9.
[WorkedStep] Both have 1 whole, so compare 1/3 and 2/9. Split each third into 3 equal pieces. The whole now has 3 × 3 = 9 pieces, and our 1/3 has 1 × 3 = 3 of them:
[WorkedStep] 1/3 = (1 × 3)/(3 × 3) = 3/9. The pieces are smaller, but the amount is unchanged.
[WorkedStep] Now 2/9 < 3/9, so 1 2/9 < 1 3/9. The smaller original fraction is 11/9.
[WorkedStep] The shared bottom number, 9, is a common denominator. It makes the pieces the same size, so we can compare how many we have.
[WorkedStep] Multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12, … . Multiples of 9: 9, 18, 27, … . The first number in both lists is 9. This is the least common multiple (LCM): the smallest positive multiple shared by 3 and 9.
Your turn after Example 1
[Question] Which is smaller, 23/18 or 7/6?
Check your method
[WorkedStep] We can also compare 23/18 and 7/6 directly. Since 6 × 3 = 18, multiply both parts of 7/6 by 3:
[WorkedStep] 7/6 = (7 × 3)/(6 × 3) = 21/18.
[WorkedStep] Since 21/18 < 23/18, the smaller fraction is 7/6.
[WorkedStep] We have compared two fractions. In the next question, we will put three fractions in ascending order. That means smallest first, then the next larger, then the greatest.
