## Multiplying and Dividing Decimals
Let’s multiply a decimal by a whole number. We can count tenths first: for example, 1.2 means 12 tenths. Four lots of 12 tenths make 48 tenths, or 4.8. Counting the place-value units helps us put the decimal point in the right place.
Example 22
[Question] Estimate without a calculator. Then evaluate.
[Question] (a)  10.2 × 3
[Question] (b)  3.4 × 20
Let’s work it through
[WorkedStep] (a) For 10.2 × 3, estimate with 10 × 3 = 30.
[WorkedStep] Now count the tenths in 10.2. Its 10 wholes contain 10 × 10 = 100 tenths. Add the 2 tenths after the point: 100 + 2 = 102 tenths.
[WorkedStep] Multiply: 102 × 3 = 306 tenths. Change back to a decimal: 300 tenths = 30 wholes, and 6 tenths = 0.6.
[WorkedStep] So 10.2 × 3 = 30 + 0.6 = 30.6.
[WorkedStep] Check: 30.6 ÷ 3 = 10.2.
[WorkedStep] (b)  Estimate: 3 × 20 = 60.
[WorkedStep] Use 20 = 10 × 2, so 3.4 × 20 = (3.4 × 10) × 2.
[WorkedStep] First 3.4 × 10 = 34. The 3 ones become 3 tens, or 30; the 4 tenths become 4 ones. Together, 30 + 4 = 34.
[WorkedStep] Then 34 × 2 = 68. So 3.4 × 20 = 68.
Your turn after Example 22
[Question] Estimate without a calculator. Then evaluate.
[Question] (a)  1.2 × 4
[Question] (b)  5.3 × 50
Check your method
[WorkedStep] (a)  Estimate: 1 × 4 = 4. Twelve tenths × 4 = 48 tenths, so 1.2 × 4 = 4.8.
[WorkedStep] (b)  Estimate: 5 × 50 = 250. Since 50 = 5 × 10, calculate 5.3 × 5 = 26.5, then 26.5 × 10 = 265.
