## Changing the way we count
Let’s change 2 3/4 into a fraction. Imagine 2 whole pies and 3/4 of another pie. In the next picture, each full rectangle represents 1 pie cut into 4 equal parts. Each small part is one quarter, 1/4, of a pie.
Start with the 2 whole pies. Each pie has 4 quarters, so 2 × 4 = 8 quarters.
Now include the 3 quarters from the third pie: 8 + 3 = 11 quarters.

We have 11 quarter-pieces. Write 11 on top. Keep 4 underneath because each piece is still 1/4 of a pie:
2 3/4 = (2 × 4 + 3)/4 = (8 + 3)/4 = 11/4.
The arrows in the picture show the order: multiply the whole number 2 by the denominator 4; then add the numerator 3. The denominator stays 4.
Now let’s go back from 11/4 to a mixed number. Four quarter-pieces make 1 whole pie. Eight quarter-pieces make 2 whole pies: 2 × 4 = 8.
From our 11 quarter-pieces, we have used 8. There are 11 − 8 = 3 quarter-pieces left. So 11/4 = 2 + 3/4 = 2 3/4.
We can write the division as 11 ÷ 4 = 2 remainder 3. Here, 2 counts the whole pies; the remainder 3 counts the quarter-pieces left.
Check: 2 × 4 + 3 = 11 quarter-pieces again. If we had 12/4, all 12 pieces would make whole pies: 12 ÷ 4 = 3, so 12/4 = 3.
We can now write the same amount as a mixed number or an improper fraction. Let’s use that to compare two amounts.
